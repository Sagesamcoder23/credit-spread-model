Credit Spread Valuation Model

Is corporate credit cheap or expensive right now, and has that kind of reading ever paid off? I built this model in Excel to answer both questions, and then to test whether my own answer holds up.

Data through Oct 20, 2026 9,602 trading days, 1990 to 2026 Excel · VBA · Bloomberg BDH · FRED Built by Samuel Aoulou, Finance BBA, Coles College of Business

01The short version

What the model does, what it says today, and what the evidence says about trusting it.

The question
Are investors being paid more or less than usual to take corporate credit risk?
I measure option-adjusted spreads (OAS) for investment grade, BBB, A and high yield bonds against their own history, using a z-score and a percentile rank. A reading has to be extreme on both before it counts.
Today's read
IG is neutral. High yield screens cheap.
Does it work?
Partly. The "rich" signal leans the right way. The "cheap" signal does not have enough evidence.
After tight-spread (RICH) days, LQD trailed duration-matched Treasuries by about 0.46% over the next 3 months versus a normal day, and the sign held in 10 of 12 settings. Neither side kept its sign in both halves of history, so I treat it as a lean, not a forecast.

What the history shows

CHEAP comes in clusters: 1998 to 2002, 2007 to 2009, 2015 to 2016 and 2020. RICH dominates the calm stretches, such as 1993 to 1996, 2003 to 2004, 2013 to 2014, 2017 and 2024 to 2025.

A year like 2008 was CHEAP for the whole year while spreads kept widening. That is why CHEAP is read as "paid more than usual" and not as "about to tighten".

The left chart shows why the model reports two percentiles. Against the last 3 years, high yield screens wide. Against all 36 years, the IG, BBB, A and high yield readings all sit in the lower half, and the full history includes crisis periods like 2008 and 2020.

The clustering is also the reason the backtest counts independent tests and not just days.

04How the signal works

The rules are simple on purpose. Every step is a formula you can click on in the workbook, and every threshold sits on one Inputs sheet.

Measure distance from normal (z-score)

How many standard deviations today's spread sits from its 756-day average. Under 1 is ordinary, 1 to 2 is notable, over 2 is rare.

Rank today against the window (percentile)

The share of days in the window when spreads were tighter than today. This is a second filter so one wide day in a quiet period is not enough.

Both must agree

CHEAP needs z ≥ +1.0 and percentile ≥ 80%. RICH is the mirror image. Anything else is NEUTRAL. CHEAP means you are paid more than usual, not that spreads will tighten.

Check for confirmation

A signal in IG is stronger when BBB says the same thing. Breadth counts how many of the six series agree, and one-month momentum shows whether spreads are still moving the same way.

Size the opportunity

If IG spreads went back to their average, what would that do to a bond fund's price? Price change ≈ −duration × change in spread. With LQD's 7.54-year duration, today's gap is worth about +0.10%.

The actual formulas (Calc sheet, IG row)

Z-score =IF(D9=0,0,(B9-C9)/D9) Percentile =PERCENTRANK(window, B9) Signal =IF(AND(E9>=Inputs!B6, F9>=Inputs!B7),"CHEAP", IF(AND(E9<=-Inputs!B6, F9<=1-Inputs!B7),"RICH","NEUTRAL")) Conviction =IF(H9="NEUTRAL","No IG signal", IF(H10=H9,"Confirmed "&H9,"Unconfirmed "&H9)) Price eff. =-Inputs!B8*(C9-B9)/10000

The window is built with INDEX(Data!B:B,start):INDEX(Data!B:B,end), so it resizes automatically when new data is loaded. Nothing is hardcoded to a row number.

Why use two filters instead of just the z-score?
A z-score assumes spreads are roughly bell-shaped. They are not. They have long right tails from crises. In a window that contains one big spike, the standard deviation gets inflated and a meaningful move can look small. The percentile is rank-based and doesn't care about the shape of the distribution. Requiring both means a reading has to be unusual by distance and by rank.
What is OAS, and why use it instead of yield?
Option-adjusted spread is the extra yield a corporate bond pays over a Treasury, after removing the value of features like call options. Yield mixes two things together: the level of interest rates and the pay for credit risk. OAS isolates the credit piece, which is what this model is trying to value. 100 bps = 1 percentage point.
Why a 756-day lookback?
It is about three years of trading days, long enough to cover more than one market mood but short enough to reflect the current regime. It is also what FRED provides for free on these ICE BofA series. The Robustness section tests 504 days, 1,260 days and all history to check that the result doesn't depend on this one choice.

05Does the signal work?

I tested the exact same rule on every day since LQD started trading. For each day I asked: over the next 63 trading days (about 3 months), did investment grade credit beat Treasuries with the same interest rate risk?

Edge vs an average day (63-day forward excess return)

If the rule works, CHEAP should sit above zero (credit outperforms after wide spreads) and RICH below zero (credit lags after tight spreads).

How I isolate the credit bet

LQD (corporate bonds) moves with both interest rates and credit spreads. To strip out the rate piece I subtract 1.10 dollars of IEF (Treasuries) for every dollar of LQD. That ratio is LQD's duration (7.54) divided by IEF's (6.85), so a rate move hits both sides about equally and what's left is the credit return.

Excess = LQD fwd return − (7.54 / 6.85) × IEF fwd return

Edge is a bucket's average excess return minus the all-days average. LQD earns extra yield on a typical day, so beating zero isn't enough. A signal has to beat a normal day.

No look-ahead: every historical signal uses only the data available on that day, through a rolling window and a 126-day warm-up.

Why "independent tests" matter more than days
Each day looks 63 days ahead, so back-to-back days share almost the same future. 898 CHEAP days is really about 898 ÷ 63 ≈ 14 separate looks at the market. I set a bar of 20 independent tests before a bucket is called Usable. Below that it is marked Thin, and its average should be read as an anecdote. Most backtests skip this step, and it is the easiest way to fool yourself with a big-looking sample.

What I take from this

The RICH side is the more believable half: tight spreads were followed by weaker credit returns in 10 of 12 testable settings. But it flipped sign between the two halves of history, and the CHEAP side is split 8 to 8 with almost no settings clearing the evidence bar. My honest conclusion is that spread valuation is useful context for the portfolio team, not a trading rule on its own. Reaching that conclusion, rather than presenting the best-looking cell, was the point of building the test.

Three data routes, one switch

Analysts without a Bloomberg terminal can paste free FRED data. With a terminal, BDH formulas pull 36 years of history. The Data sheet reads whichever source Inputs!B11 names, so everything downstream is identical no matter where the numbers came from.

VBA snapshot macro

Bloomberg formulas break on any computer without the add-in. I wrote an UpdateSnapshot macro that copies a live pull into static sheets so the team can open the model anywhere. It refuses to overwrite good data with an empty or broken pull, flags series with mismatched row counts, and asks before saving anything suspicious.

Guardrails built in

A units check catches FRED data pasted in percent instead of bps. A red banner shows when sample data is loaded, so synthetic numbers never reach a presentation. Color coding follows banking convention: blue = input, black = formula, green = link.

08Limits and what's next

What this model does not tell you

  • Spreads can stay rich or cheap for years. A signal is a mild lean, not a prediction.
  • A calm 3-year window can make today look extreme. Always compare with the all-history percentile.
  • Buckets marked Thin rest on a handful of independent episodes.
  • The backtest ignores trading costs, and LQD is a proxy for the IG index, not the index itself.
  • It makes no portfolio or weighting recommendation. It is one input for the team's decisions.

Next steps

  • Port to Python. The Calc rows map directly to pandas rolling mean, rolling std and rank, so the model can plug into our fund's existing portfolio optimizer.
  • Make it forward-looking. Run a principal component analysis on the yield curve to find what drives curve moves, then shock the curve and measure the effect at each duration.
  • Add macro context. Use inflation and economic data to adjust assumptions and help screen for new holdings.
